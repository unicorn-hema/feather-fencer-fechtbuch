# The Five Words — Zettel 17–20

<p class="fff-deck">A reading of <em>Vor, Nach, Stark, Schwach,</em> and <em>Indes</em> as the tactical foundation of Liechtenauer's art — and of their use in modern fencing.</p>

## TL;DR

The Five Words are, to me, the very core of the Liechtenauer tradition's tactical framework. RDL explains them in relatively broad terms, while 3227a defines them much more narrowly — even though the later plays in both traditions seem to follow the same broader tactical logic.

In my modern reading, the Five Words provide a form of **decision compression**. Instead of consciously solving every variable in an exchange, you look for the information that matters at that moment and use it to select an appropriate response from a small set of trained continuations. The information may change several times during a single action. At the same time, you can simplify the problem indirectly by creating threats that restrict the opponent's reasonable responses. The result is not a perfect decision every time, but a good decision available at fencing speed.

The first two couplets introduce *Vor*, *Nach*, *Schwach*, *Stark*, and *Indes*. The final two connect understanding them with practical skill and warn against fear. Neither the word *Indes* nor the warning against fright is unambiguous enough to settle its full tactical implications from the verse alone; that is the work of the glosses.[^zettel-language]

## 2. Historical glosses

<p class="fff-label">Source summaries · attributed editorial wording</p>

### 2.1 GNM Hs. 3227a — anonymous commentary

The commentator eventually returns to these verses and explicitly calls *Vor, Nach, Schwach, Stark,* and *Indes* the **five words** on which Liechtenauer's fencing rests. They are described as its foundation and core. But the explanation proceeds mainly through one connected sequence: seek the *Vorschlag* (first blow), then continue with a *Nachschlag* whether the first attack hits or misses, and keep attacking without pause so that the opponent has no opportunity to begin their own action. In that summary, *Vor* and *Nach* are expressly related to *Vorschlag* and *Nachschlag*.[^3227a]

*Indes* is not restricted to feeling the bind. The commentator relates it, first, to making the follow-up during the opponent's defence against the first attack; second, to *Fühlen*, sensing whether the opponent is strong or weak on the sword and acting accordingly. Thus the text offers both a continuation during an ongoing defence and a contact-dependent choice of action. Its earlier technical teaching also tells the fencer to distinguish hard or soft, strong or weak resistance in the bind; elsewhere it recognises that the blade gives greater mechanical advantage nearer the hilt. These are not presented as a single formal taxonomy.[^3227a-contact]

### 2.2 RDL — Ringeck, pseudo-Peter von Danzig, and Lew

**Shared teaching.** All three commentaries treat the terms in verses 17–20 as foundational. They instruct the fencer to obtain *Vor* by presenting a cut or thrust that makes the opponent defend, and then to work quickly into further openings rather than allowing the opponent to act freely. They also describe the other side of the relationship: when the opponent attacks first and I must defend, I should work *Indes* during that defence towards an opening. They distinguish the strong and weak portions of the blade and connect *Indes* with the choice of what to do in contact. The terminology and amount of explanation differ, however, so the three texts should not be treated as a single voice.[^rdl-common]

**Ringeck.** His exposition makes the transition from *Nach* back to *Vor* particularly clear. When the opponent attacks first and forces a defence, the fencer is told to work *Indes* with that defence to the nearest opening and hit before the opponent completes the action; the next paragraph describes this as winning *Vor* while the opponent remains in *Nach*. Ringeck then explains the strong and weak by the sword's length: from the hilt to approximately the middle is strong, from the middle towards the point weak (later, under *Fühlen*, he describes recognising a hard or soft bind at the moment the swords meet and working *Indes* to an opening.)[^ringeck]

**Pseudo-Peter von Danzig.** The opening gloss first names the terms as the foundation from which the art arises. It then gives separate practical explanations: take *Vor* with an attack that requires a parry; if the opponent comes first, break their *Vor* with one's *Nach* by working *Indes* towards the nearest opening during the defence. The strong and weak of the sword are explicitly defined geometrically. (In the later treatment of *Fühlen* and *Indes*, the author gives an unusually wide range of applications, including *Duplieren*, *Mutieren*, *Durchwechseln*, cutting, wrestling, and disarming. That broad list matters when considering whether *Indes* should be confined to a single type of action.)[^danzig]

**Lew.** Lew follows much of the same exposition: the introductory passage names the fundamental terms; the subsequent instructions distinguish taking *Vor* by forcing the opponent to defend from using *Indes* in one's own defence to take the opponent's *Vor* away with *Nach*. Lew also defines the strong and weak as parts of the blade and, later, connects *Indes* to *Fühlen* and to a similarly broad range of technical continuations. For the argument of this chapter, Lew reinforces the shared tactical account rather than providing an entirely different definition.[^lew]

### 2.3 Comparison

RDL states the reciprocal *Vor/Nach* relationship — including the possibility of taking *Vor* from an opponent who acted first — much more directly. This is a difference in explicit exposition; whether it implies a difference in the underlying fencing is a separate question, taken up in the author's interpretation.

## 3. My historical interpretation

<p class="fff-label">Author's reading · historical context</p>

The difference between 3227a and RDL is hard to miss when it comes to the Five Words. Both traditions regard them as fundamental to the art (and both apply similar tactical logic in later plays), yet their explanations could hardly be more different.

RDL gives me something I recognise as a tactical framework. *Vor* and *Nach* describe who is leading the exchange and who is reacting to the problem presented. *Stark* and *Schwach* provide one of the most common sources of information for deciding what to do next. *Indes* allows the relationship to change while the exchange is still unfolding. The presentation is not always as explicit as a modern technical manual, but the structure is there.

3227a, by comparison, explains the Five Words remarkably poorly. I don't think its author misunderstood the fencing — when I look at the later plays, I repeatedly see the same tactical logic that RDL presents. What 3227a seems unable to do is put that logic into words. Interestingly, the manuscript itself may offer a clue. Earlier, its commentator remarks that the art of the sword is **better shown by hand than explained in writing**. It's hard not to remember his own words here. If the Five Words really are the foundation of the art, their explicit explanation in 3227a leaves an extraordinary amount for the reader to work out from the subsequent plays.

### Vor and Nach (Before and After)

In RDL, I read *Vor* and *Nach* primarily as initiative — or, more simply, as the relationship between action and reaction. The distinction is not merely chronological; it's not necessarily about who happened to move first. The difference is tactical: who is creating the situation, and who has to respond to it? If my action presents a credible problem that you must solve, I am in *Vor*. If your action creates a problem for me and I am responding to it, I am in *Nach*. The relationship can change, but at any given moment during an unfolding exchange, it's remarkably easy to understand who plays which role.

3227a explains the pair through a much more concrete sequence. *Vor* is tied to *Vorschlag*, the first attack, and *Nach* to *Nachschlag*, the follow-up actions that come after it. The fencer closes distance and attacks as soon as the opponent can be reached. If that first attack is displaced, they continue immediately with another attack, and then another if necessary, without letting the opponent begin their own work. And that's it: the whole of *Vor* and *Nach*. This may be useful fencing advice, but it's a rather bland explanation of such fundamental concepts. RDL describes a relationship that can apply throughout an exchange; 3227a describes a straightforward attacking sequence. I firmly believe that this is mainly a difference in explanation, not in the underlying fencing. As I mentioned earlier, the later plays in 3227a repeatedly apply the broader RDL-style logic, even though the manuscript never names it so clearly.[^3227a-counter]

### Stark and Schwach (Strong and Weak)

In its explanation of the Five Words, 3227a presents *Stark* and *Schwach* in a dynamic sense. It ties them directly to *Fühlen*: feel whether the opponent is strong or weak on the sword, then act accordingly. Elsewhere, however, 3227a also recognises the geometrical advantage of the stronger part of the blade. Its understanding is not limited to the behaviour of the bind.

RDL explicitly defines *Stark* and *Schwach* as parts of the sword: the strong runs from the hilt to the middle of the blade, the weak from there towards the point. As a geometrical definition, that's quite straightforward. Yet the glosses do not stay within that definition when they describe actual fencing. They repeatedly examine whether the opponent is strong or weak on the sword — whether they press, resist, or yield. The manuscripts often use the terms *Hart* and *Weich* here, but sometimes return to *Stark* and *Schwach*.

Both groups of glosses therefore work with the geometry of the blades and with the dynamic behaviour of their contact, even if their explanations emphasise different things.

To a modern reader, these may look like separate categories that the glossators are carelessly mixing. I am not convinced medieval fencers would have seen it that way. Geometry and active pressure are different mechanical factors, but both can influence what happens when the blades meet in similar ways. The glossators can distinguish them, and sometimes clearly do. Yet in practice the immediate question may simply be how the contact behaves and what that allows me to do next. That is why I think it is important to recognise both meanings rather than force every occurrence of *Stark* and *Schwach* into one rigid definition.

### Indes (In Between, or Meantime)

*Indes* is where I find the contrast between the two source traditions most striking. In RDL, I understand it quite literally as acting **in the meantime**: doing something while another relevant action is still happening. It works in *Vor*: I attack and, while the opponent is forming a parry, I change the trajectory of my cut (rather than necessarily starting a completely new action). I can also act to prevent the opponent from using their own *Indes* to take my initiative away. And it works in *Nach*: the opponent attacks and, while that attack is developing, I answer with an action that deals with the incoming threat while presenting a credible threat at the same time — typically through a *Meisterhau* — and take the *Vor* from them.

This is what makes *Indes* so important. Without it, *Vor* and *Nach* would describe a largely linear exchange. One fencer gains initiative; the other reacts; and that relationship continues until the first fencer succeeds or makes a serious mistake. *Indes* makes the structure dynamic. It can help me develop or preserve the initiative I already have, or change the relationship before the opponent's action is complete. This does not mean that every use of *Indes* changes who has the *Vor*.

The explicit explanation in 3227a is much less satisfying. It does connect *Indes* with acting during the opponent's defence: the *Nachschlag* follows while the opponent is still dealing with the *Vorschlag*. It also connects *Indes* with *Fühlen*: feel whether the opponent is strong or weak in the bind, and immediately know what to do against them. I do not believe 3227a lacks the broader tactical idea found in RDL. I believe that, for whatever reason, its explicit account does not communicate the full range of the concept it clearly uses elsewhere.

### Verses 19–20

Verses 19–20 do not introduce more words, but they belong to the same lesson. I read the final warning against fear as a demand for decisiveness. The Liechtenauer tradition builds its approach around taking initiative, maintaining it when possible, and recovering it when lost. None of that works if the fencer is so afraid of all the possible responses that they never commit to an action. But the recommended mindset is not the same as saying that it does not matter whether you get hit. It is a warning against allowing fear and hesitation to prevent you from doing the art at all.

Interestingly, the gloss preserved in the manuscript associated with Hans Medel provides a contrast to our early sources. In its explanation of *Nach*, a scribal insertion makes explicit that a fencer may not only fail to gain the *Vor*, but may also willingly choose not to take it. The text then describes waiting in *Nach* and working *Indes* against the opponent's action to recover the *Vor* once the opponent starts the exchange. The broader tactical framework remains the same, but willingly working from *Nach* is now presented as a viable option.[^medel]

## 4. My application in modern HEMA

<p class="fff-label fff-label-modern">Author's practice · present-day conditions</p>

### The Five Words as decision compression

I have already introduced the idea of **decision compression** in the Preface: fencing is simply too complicated to perceive in its entirety while doing it. Distance, timing, balance, blade position, pressure, openings, threats, footwork, possible attacks and counters, and the opponent's intentions all change at once. Given enough time, you could analyse a remarkable number of variables. But in the middle of an unfolding exchange, you can't consciously calculate them all.

What I find remarkable about the Liechtenauer tradition is how aggressively it simplifies that problem. The Five Words do not describe everything that is happening. They help us recognise what matters *now*, while the individual plays give us trained, relatively optimised responses to the situations that follow. This is also one of my possible explanations for the longevity of the tradition: interpretations and applications could change, but the underlying (compressed) decision framework remained compact and useful.

### Vor and Nach: one question first

The first compression is *Vor* and *Nach*. At any moment, I need to answer one simple question: **am I forcing the opponent to solve my problem, or am I solving theirs?** I do not need a complete theory of *tempo* in that instant. I need to know whether I am creating the situation or responding to one that has already been created for me.

That is why I want *Vor*. When I present a credible threat, the opponent must spend time and attention dealing with it. Their options are constrained by what I have already done. It does not make my next action automatically correct or safe, but I believe the price of a mistake is generally much higher for the fencer who must answer an existing threat than for the one presenting it.

There is also an indirect side to this compression. Through a credible threat, the choice of distance, and control of the line, I can restrict the responses that are reasonably available to the opponent. I cannot prevent them from doing something unexpected or tactically foolish, but I can try to make the likely reactions fewer and easier to read. In that sense, I am not just simplifying my own decisions; I am also limiting theirs. So, in the end, I am trying to shape the exchange so that each decision is as simple as possible in the first place. (This may also explain why Liechtenauer's tradition prefers to close the distance so aggressively in *Zufechten* — but that is barely more than a personal impression.)

### Stark and Schwach: one useful piece of information

Once the weapons meet, the exchange quickly becomes mechanically complicated. But I don't want to calculate every detail of the bind. I'm interested only in the particular piece of information that tells me what to do next. I believe this is how Liechtenauer's plays repeatedly simplify the decision: **at each decision point, one input becomes decisive**. That input can change as the exchange develops, but I deal with one immediate question at a time. And once the blades have met, *Stark* or *Schwach* is very often the decisive distinction.

I enter, the opponent responds, and the swords bind. I feel the contact and select from a small number of trained, optimised continuations. My action creates a new situation, from which I take the next relevant piece of information and make the next decision. This can happen several times within a single exchange, without requiring me to analyse the entire fight consciously all at once. Nor does it require a bind: in *Nachreisen*, for example, I can track whether an opponent's attack still threatens me and act when it has passed. The decisive question remains simple even as its answer changes.

This pattern will become especially clear in the individual *Meisterhäue* and other principal pieces.

### Indes: the system becomes dynamic

The historical interpretation above explains why I regard *Indes* as the concept that breaks the otherwise linear sequence. In my model, a proper *Indes* action should always follow two principles at once: **my point should threaten the opponent** and **my crossguard should cover me**. The difference between applying this principle in *Vor* and *Nach* is only in my priorities: in *Vor*, I care about the point before the crossguard; in *Nach*, I care about the crossguard before the point.

That completes the decision-compression framework as I use it. *Vor/Nach* tells me the current relationship; *Stark/Schwach* often supplies the information needed to choose a continuation; and *Indes* lets me work within the ongoing action regardless of whether I am currently in *Vor* or *Nach*.

### A practical analysis tool: Vor-like, Nach-like and Indes-like behaviour

There is an additional tool I use to make ongoing exchanges easier to read. The starting point is to distinguish **the objective tactical state** from **the way the opponent actually behaves**. The two do not always correlate. I may have *Vor* because I am presenting a threat that should force a response, yet the opponent may behave as though *they* have *Vor* and attack instead of defending. That is one obvious route to a double.

To make this easier to recognise (and deal with), I describe three types of *intuitive behaviour*. These are not historical definitions and not permanent classifications of fencers; they are practical labels for what I can observe:

> **A tool for reading the opponent**
>
> - **Vor-like:** they react by moving their blade towards the opponent, directly threatening them.
> - **Nach-like:** they initiate an action with their blade directed primarily towards the opponent's blade, seeking to close or deflect an attacking line rather than presenting an immediate threat of their own.
> - **Indes-like:** their point threatens the opponent while their crossguard simultaneously closes the line of the opponent's attack, regardless of the current tactical state.
>
> A fencer may switch between these behaviours, even within a single exchange; but many people tend to prefer and apply one of these approaches over the others. The purpose of the labels is to recognise a tendency and prepare an appropriate countermeasure, not to assign a fixed personality type.

The practical consequences matter more to me than the labels. Against someone who consistently behaves *Vor-like*, I cannot assume that my credible attack will make them defend. Their preferred response is to attack even when objectively in *Nach*; often, though not always, they aim at the hands or forearms.

Against a consistently *Nach-like* opponent, if I see them advance or attack, I expect a feint or provocation rather than an immediate threat, so I usually answer with an attack of my own. Such opponents generally seek to provoke an attack they can parry before committing themselves. Since I expect that parry, I can adjust my attack accordingly — making their defence more difficult or, ideally, bypassing it entirely.

And against someone who repeatedly behaves *Indes-like*, I must account for the possibility that they will attack and close my line at the same time, regardless of who initiated the exchange. I should therefore work more with feints or provocations, even when I see a credible opening; and it is worth noting that such behaviour often (though not always) reduces the reach of their threats.

The point is to recognise the opponent's actual behaviour and then apply an appropriate response from the Liechtenauer tradition based on their type, rather than assuming they will respond in the way the current tactical state suggests they should.

### Fear, uncertainty and doubles

The final verse becomes particularly interesting in modern HEMA because our environment changes the cost of being wrong. An opponent can always do something tactically foolish: attack into an attack, ignore a threat, or make some other poor decision. In modern competition, however, they can repeat such behaviour and survive it; some rulesets may even reward such actions with points.

The point is that you can't account for every possible response before committing. If you try, you will never start any action. This is where the old warning against fear still holds. I should make every action as sound and clean as possible, constrain the opponent's likely responses, and minimise the chance of being hit. Yet I must accept that I can still be struck even if I do everything correctly. The existence of that possibility cannot be allowed to stop me from acting.

That does not mean doubles are fine. It means you have to accept a degree of uncertainty. A fencer's job is to make the opponent's successful response difficult and unlikely, not to wait until it becomes impossible.

### A map, not GPS

Overall, I think of the Five Words — and, more broadly, Liechtenauer's approach to fencing — as a map rather than turn-by-turn GPS navigation. GPS is excellent if I want to get from one precise address to another: it gives me a route and tells me what to do at each junction. But if I want to understand the shape of Europe, a detailed route is useless. I need a map that deliberately leaves out detail so that the larger relationships become visible.

The Five Words work like that map. They do not prescribe the optimal technique for every situation; instead, they help me orient myself in the broader tactical landscape. The simplification necessarily sacrifices detail, but that is not automatically a weakness. Precise decision processes may be hard to execute at fencing speed, or may lack instructions for a particular (unusual) situation. A decision made through the Five Words may not be perfect, but it should be available (almost) instantly. **That, to me, is their real strength.**
