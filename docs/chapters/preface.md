# Preface — Zettel 1–8

<p class="fff-deck">Liechtenauer and his glossators use these opening words to welcome the reader into the art. I want to use the same doorway to explain how I intend to read the material that follows.</p>

The verses conventionally numbered 1–8 appear to be a general preface to Liechtenauer's wider art rather than an introduction written specifically for the long sword. That makes them particularly convenient here. The historical text is opening its art; I am going to use the same opportunity to open this fechtbuch.

## TL;DR

The surviving Liechtenauer tradition does not begin by giving us a catalogue of techniques. It begins by placing fencing within a broader art, and shortly afterwards gives us a small set of concepts through which that art is to be understood: *Vor, Nach, Stark, Schwach,* and *Indes*. I read those concepts as a fundamental simplification of the otherwise overwhelming complexity of sword fighting. In particular, I follow the RDL glosses in reading *Vor* and *Nach* primarily as a relationship of initiative — or, more literally, action and reaction — rather than as an early version of the later Italian concept of *tempo*.

The rest of this chapter is not a reconstruction of verses 1–8, but an explanation of the lens through which FFF will read everything that follows. Two assumptions are particularly important. First, the surviving texts give us only fragments of the world in which this fencing existed; even a technically convincing reconstruction cannot restore context that was never written down. Second, I read the *Zettel* as something like a curriculum map for a “course in the art of fencing according to Master Liechtenauer”, and the glosses as textbooks or teaching commentaries for that course. In that reading, many of the plays are teaching tools rather than literal recipes for complete fights.

*Translation notes*[^zettel-notes]

The preface is broader than the long sword. It speaks to a young knight, places fencing alongside wrestling and other weapons, and mixes practical advice with questions of honour, conduct and measure. The exact reading of some lines — especially verse 7, and to a lesser extent what *length and measure* encompasses in verse 8 — is less secure than a smooth English translation can make it appear. None of this materially affects my interpretation below.

## 2. Historical glosses

<p class="fff-label">Source summaries · attributed editorial wording</p>

### 2.1 GNM Hs. 3227a — anonymous commentary

3227a treats the opening as a general introduction to unarmoured fencing on foot and expands it with considerably more material of its own. Its commentary combines practical advice with questions of judgement and conduct: act with reason, keep the right measure, distinguish earnest fencing from play, and do not mistake recklessness for skill. More importantly for this book, the manuscript repeatedly reminds us that fencing is not exhausted by the written text. At one point the commentator says directly that the art cannot be explained through writing as well as it can be shown by hand. Practice is not an optional supplement to the text; the author explicitly treats it as necessary to understanding the art.[^gloss-3227a]

This matters because 3227a later gives unusually explicit conceptual summaries of Liechtenauer's art. It says that fencing depends on five words — *Vor, Nach, Stark, Schwach,* and *Indes* — and calls them its basis, core and foundation. Its explanation of *Vor* and *Nach* is distinctive: it equates them with *Vorschlag* and *Nachschlag*, the leading attack and the attack that follows it. Once the first attack (*Vorschlag*) has been executed, the fencer should continue without pause with a second, third, fourth or further action (*Nachschläge*) so that the opponent never gets to begin their own work.[^gloss-3227a]

### 2.2 RDL — Ringeck, pseudo-Peter von Danzig and Lew

Ringeck, pseudo-Peter von Danzig and Lew preserve a closely related body of commentary, although they are not identical witnesses and should not be treated as three independent confirmations of the same source. Their prose introductions describe Liechtenauer's teaching as deliberately compressed or concealed and present the gloss as an explanation of that material. Ringeck's formulation is especially revealing: the explanation is offered so that a man **who already knows how to fence properly** may understand the *Zettel*. Whatever we make of the rhetoric of secrecy surrounding the art, these texts plainly do not behave like self-contained modern manuals for somebody learning fencing from scratch.[^gloss-rdl]

RDL also gives a different explicit account of *Vor* and *Nach* from 3227a. If the fencer comes first with an attack that forces the opponent to defend, the fencer has the *Vor* and should continue working so that the opponent does not reach their own action. If the opponent comes first and the fencer must answer that play, the fencer is working in the *Nach*; the glosses describe that work as breaking what is being done and, if successful, taking the *Vor* away from the opponent. The difference from 3227a is real and I do not intend to harmonise it away. Despite that difference, I read both approaches as sharing an important tactical logic: initiative does not automatically expire merely because an attack has been defended.[^gloss-rdl]

## 3. My historical interpretation

<p class="fff-label">Author's reading · the conceptual frame of the art</p>

Fencing is far too complicated to perceive in its entirety while actually doing it. Two people can move forward and backward, change measure, attack, counterattack, defend, bind, disengage, feint, provoke and alter their intentions continuously, while each movement changes the possibilities available to both of them. In reality, everyone is facing the same physical problem with broadly the same set of tools available. Yet the human mind cannot keep up with that problem in its entirety at fighting speed. Historical fencing systems therefore do something more important than merely supply collections of techniques: they decide how to simplify the fight enough for the fencer to comprehend it in real time.

I think this is one of the most important lenses through which to understand why fencing traditions can look so different while working with the same human bodies and broadly similar weapons: they simplify the fight differently. Later Italian fencing develops an extraordinarily useful conceptual system around *tempo*.[^tempo] We use that vocabulary so naturally today that it is easy to project it backwards, but the Liechtenauer glosses did not organise or describe their teaching through the later Italian concept of *tempo*. If I use the word *tempo* anywhere in FFF, I am knowingly applying later terminology to a historical context in which it did not belong, simply because that terminology and analytical approach can still be useful for a modern reader.

The Liechtenauer tradition gives us another framework. Shortly after the preface, the *Zettel* introduces *Vor* and *Nach*, then *Stark, Schwach,* and *Indes*. RDL presents these concepts as the basis from which the foundation of the art grows; 3227a is even more explicit and calls them five words on which the art depends.[^gloss-3227a][^gloss-rdl] I am deliberately not going to unpack those here in detail — *Indes* in particular opens a much larger problem that deserves its own treatment. The important point for now is that the tradition itself gives exceptional weight to this compact conceptual framework. I use **the Five Words** as a convenient umbrella term for what I regard as a *fundamental simplification of fencing* in Liechtenauer's tradition.

> **Why I follow RDL on Vor and Nach**
>
> RDL treats *Vor* and *Nach* as a tactical relationship between the fencers. *Vor* describes the side creating the immediate fencing problem: I act in such a way that you must deal with me. *Nach* describes the side responding to that problem: you have acted, and I must now work against what you are doing. Time is obviously part of this relationship — one action has preceded another — but it is not the whole relationship. The most useful modern shorthand I have found is **initiative**, or more literally **action and reaction**.
>
> That does not make *Nach* passive, nor does it make *Vor* a token that is spent after one attack. A reaction may itself strike, threaten or overturn the exchange. Conversely, RDL tells the fencer who has gained *Vor* to keep working through the opponent's defence so that the opponent cannot begin their own work.
>
> 3227a explains the terms differently, equating *Vor/Nach* with *Vorschlag/Nachschlag*. I do not want to erase that difference; but when FFF speaks about *Vor* and *Nach* as initiative, I am consciously following RDL. At the same time, 3227a reaches a closely related practical demand through its own terminology: follow the first action with another, and another, so that the opponent remains occupied and never reaches their own attack. That shared tactical logic is one reason I find the RDL definition so productive for the rest of this book.
>
> This conceptual distinction will matter throughout FFF. The surviving Liechtenauer tradition does not frame the exchange in Italian *tempi*; it gives us *Vor* and *Nach*. I may sometimes describe the same physical exchange using *tempo* because it is an excellent analytical tool, but when I do so the terminology and the model are mine, not his.

## 4. How I read the sources

<p class="fff-label fff-label-modern">Author's interpretive framework</p>

### The missing world

Before trying to reconstruct anything from source texts, there is an uncomfortable fact to accept: **we have the texts, but we do not know their world**. We do not know much of the context their intended fifteenth-century readers already understood without needing to be told. We do not know what was demonstrated physically yet never written down. We often do not know the exact social context in which a particular play or approach was expected to be used, how those contexts changed over time, or how much the practice of one generation differed from that of another. The sources themselves tell us that something is missing: 3227a privileges demonstration and practice over words alone, while the RDL glosses address readers who are already expected to know how to fence.[^gloss-3227a][^gloss-rdl]

Occasionally the darkness opens for a moment. A Prague ordinance from 1597 regulates public *Fechtschulen* on Sundays and religious holidays and describes the practice in the context of exercise for the young. That is an extraordinary glimpse of a living fencing culture, with events potentially occurring several times a week, public and socially embedded enough to require civic regulation.[^prague-1597] But it is one glimpse, from one city, at one particular time. It cannot tell us what Prague looked like a century earlier, let alone what a different city looked like.

Worse, even some very basic questions remain unsettled. Modern scholarship still argues over the dating of 3227a, the period in which Liechtenauer himself lived, and what kind of continuity — or discontinuity — connects the earliest surviving material to the later manuscript cluster. Maciej Talaga's recent work is valuable to me here less for the particular chronology he proposes than for what the debate itself demonstrates: even the basic history of how this knowledge reached us is only partially reconstructed from incomplete evidence.[^talaga-2026]

We are also missing critical information about the contexts for which this fencing was intended. Was a particular teaching primarily meant for potentially lethal duels, fights to first blood, competitive fencing, training for any of those situations, or some combination we no longer understand? We often cannot tell how broadly a given lesson was expected to transfer between such contexts — or whether the answer was even the same for 3227a and the later RDL tradition.

This does not reduce historical fencing to guesswork where every answer is equally good. Language constrains us. Manuscript relationships constrain us. Material culture constrains us. Bodies, weapons and mechanics constrain us, and practical experiment can expose an interpretation that simply does not work. But those constraints do not magically restore information that was never recorded. Several explanations may survive all of them. At best, we are moving through a dark room by matchlight: we can see some objects very clearly, infer the shape of others and avoid walking straight into what is in front of us. We should not mistake that for having seen the whole room.

> **A thought experiment: reconstructing “gunfighting”**
>
> Imagine a historian five hundred years from now trying to reconstruct our “gunfighting tradition” from whatever happens to survive. Soldiers, police tactical teams, competitive shooters, paintball and airsoft players, children with Nerf blasters, and even virtual gunfights in games all share recognisable concepts, imagery, language and patterns of action. Yet the purposes, risks, conventions and rules governing those activities are radically different.
>
> We recognise those differences immediately because we live inside the context that gives them meaning. A future historian working from scattered surviving manuals, regulations, videos and cultural debris — not to mention a few mixed-in memes, Reddit posts and clickbait YouTube videos — might not. If some kinds of evidence survived far better than others, they might even mistake an unusually well-documented fragment for the normal or dominant form of the whole thing.
>
> Historical fencing confronts us with a similar danger. We do not know the full range of fencing practices that existed in Liechtenauer's world, how common each was, how they overlapped, or exactly which of them his teaching was designed to address. The missing context is not an empty space we can simply fill with whatever seems plausible. It is a puzzle for which we may never find all the pieces, let alone solve completely.

### The syllabus hypothesis

That uncertainty makes another question unexpectedly important: **what kind of text are we actually reading?**

My answer began in a way that is worth admitting because it is almost the opposite of how a proper academic argument should begin. I did not study the evidence and derive a neat hypothesis. I first started reading the *Zettel* and glosses in a particular way because the material suddenly made much more sense to me.

I treat the *Zettel* as something like a fencing-course syllabus — an ordered map of what should be taught — and the glosses as textbooks or teaching commentaries for that course, expanding the compressed topics into lessons and worked examples. In that reading, I do not expect every play to be a complete recipe for a given type of exchange. Instead, I ask what a student might be intended to learn from the deliberately constrained situation being presented. The result was coherent, teachable and, when I tried to apply it in practice, unexpectedly productive.

Only afterwards did I start asking whether I had simply invented a useful modern fiction. *That order matters, because it creates an obvious confirmation-bias problem: I already liked the model before I started looking for evidence for it.* The honest response is not to rewrite the story and pretend that the evidence led me here. It is to state that bias openly and then try to break the model.

To my surprise, this theory has proved difficult to break. The long-sword material maintains a remarkably stable sequence across the major gloss traditions. Ringeck presents seventeen principal pieces beginning with the five hews; pseudo-Danzig and Lew can express the same structure as five hews followed by twelve further pieces. Much later, Medel retains the same fundamental organisation even though he knows older glosses, is perfectly willing to disagree with them, and sometimes does so openly.[^syllabus-structure] That stability could simply reflect respect for an authoritative text, so by itself it proves very little. But it is not the only indirect evidence.

In 3227a, the commentator at one point complains that the *Veller* has been written in the wrong place and says where it ought to stand instead. Apparently the location of a piece of material mattered enough to be corrected. In the mounted material (*Rossfechten*), meanwhile, a set of figures appears to encode much the same teaching as the verses while following a very different structure; it may represent an alternative sequence for studying the same material.[^syllabus-order] I find both arrangements — the same content organised differently — logically workable as teaching sequences.

None of those observations proves that Liechtenauer intended the *Zettel* as a syllabus. Taken together, however, they make that reading much harder for me to dismiss as merely a convenient modern metaphor.

The model also happens to resemble a broad pattern known from other forms of period practical education. We have evidence for artistic workshop training built around supervised practice, imitation of a master's work and, eventually, increasingly independent work under continued oversight.[^workshop-learning] We do **not** have evidence that historical fencing schools followed an identical formal three-stage programme, and I do not want to smuggle one in by analogy. What interests me is that the Liechtenauer material can be read in a strikingly similar progression:

1. **Acquire the basic frame.** The opening teaching establishes the conceptual vocabulary and fundamental rules of the art.
2. **Learn through constrained examples.** The five hews and their associated plays give the student named actions and tightly structured situations in which important relationships can be experienced and practised.
3. **Work increasingly through general principles.** The remaining pieces deal progressively with broader actions and relationships that can be applied beyond the original examples.

This is an analogy, not a reconstruction of a historical lesson plan. But the fit is hard to ignore. Even the placement of the four guards (*Huten*) and the four *Versetzen* makes sense in this reading: the text first supplies the vocabulary of the guards, then directly connects them to already introduced hews. The student is not entering an entirely new world; previously learned material is being reorganised and generalised.

I therefore want to be explicit about the epistemic status of this idea. **I cannot demonstrate that the *Zettel* was designed as a syllabus for fencing masters.** I cannot demonstrate that the glosses were meant to function as the teaching examples I see in them. Other interpretations of both the *Zettel* and the glosses can be — and are — responsible, coherent and historically well supported. FFF does not reject them. It simply does not use them as its organising principle.

### What this means for FFF

One consequence is especially important: **I read many plays as lessons, not recipes.** Imagine that Liechtenauer were teaching painting and the *Zettel* told the student to establish the outline of a face, then its dominant features, then its details. One glossator might tell the student to place the nose first and anchor the eyes and mouth around it; another might establish the eyes first, add the mouth and then fit the nose between them. Those procedures are clearly different, yet both may faithfully implement the same higher-level instruction. *A difference between glosses therefore does not automatically require one of them to be wrong.*

Nor does it automatically prove that both are equivalent expressions of an original abstract principle. Traditions change. A later teacher may reinterpret an older teaching, deliberately innovate, or preserve the name while shifting the mechanics underneath it. Medel is a particularly useful example in this regard, because he demonstrably had access to older interpretations and nevertheless sometimes chose to disagree with them openly. When historical witnesses differ, the difference itself is a form of evidence.

The practical question for FFF is therefore not always **“What is the one correct Schielhau?”** It is often **“What has to remain true for an interpretation still to be reasonably called Schielhau?”** I want to identify the constraints imposed by the *Zettel*, see which features the glosses consistently preserve, see where they vary, and distinguish a change in mechanical implementation from a change in the identity of the thing being taught. That may leave room for several historically acceptable versions of the same named action, but it does not leave room for everything to be *Schielhau* simply because we want it to be.

This distinction is fundamental. A technique can be mechanically excellent, tactically brilliant and extremely successful in modern fencing and still not be *Schielhau*. Conversely, two actions can look significantly different and still belong to the same historical category if both satisfy the constraints that define it. Practical success is evidence that something works; it is not evidence that the historical sources called it by a particular name, or even that they knew or used that action at all.

And this applies to **any interpretation**, not merely to modern reconstruction. A fifteenth-century glossator, a sixteenth-century reinterpretation and a twenty-first-century HEMA practitioner can all propose different implementations of the same compressed text. Their historical proximity gives them different evidential weight, but none becomes identical with Liechtenauer's intention merely because it exists. Every interpretation has to be tested against the evidence that survives.

That is the basic contract of this fechtbuch. I will try to keep separate what the sources actually say, what I think they mean, what I merely suspect, and how I interpret and implement those ideas in a modern fencing context. I will sometimes disagree with a historical gloss. I will sometimes prefer one branch of the tradition over another. I will sometimes use later or modern analytical concepts because they help me understand — and explain — what is happening. I consider that perfectly acceptable, provided I say openly where and when I am doing so.

Liechtenauer and his glossators use their preface to welcome us into the art that follows. I have used it rather shamelessly to explain how I intend to read that art.

Now we can start fencing.
