# Zufechten — Zettel 9–16

<p class="fff-deck">Zettel verses 9–16 of the wider <em>Common Lesson</em> (9–20): entering with a committed threat, and adapting its tactical principle to modern HEMA.</p>

*Zufechten* is an editorial chapter title, not a separate heading supplied by the Zettel.

## TL;DR

I read these verses as an introductory lesson in *Zufechten*: enter with a committed, credible cut aimed at the opponent's head or body. The glosses additionally instruct the fencer to keep the point threatening before the opponent's face or upper chest. The attack creates an immediate problem that the opponent must address. In my interpretation, defence with the blade is the central part of this **introductory scenario**, not the only possible response in an actual fight.

In modern HEMA, simply reproducing that cut does not reliably create the same tactical situation. I therefore aim to preserve the underlying principle rather than copy a fixed opening: initiate with a credible threat, elicit a response on terms that favour my next action, and exploit it while maintaining *Vor*.

### Sense translation — provisional, gloss-informed

> **9** If you wish to see the art, step with the right foot as you cut from the right.  
> **10** And with the left as you cut from the left, if strength is what you'd wield.  
> **11** Whoever follows after cuts will have little cause to rejoice in their art.  
> **12** Cut in close as you will; let no *changer* reach your shield.  
> **13** At the head, at the body; do not neglect the quick strikes (*Zeck*).  
> **14** With the whole body, fence whatever you wish to execute strongly.  
> **15** Hear what is bad: if you are right-handed, do not fence from above on the left.  
> **16** And if you are left-handed, you are likewise badly hindered on the right.

**Translation notes.** The rendering of **9–10** is deliberately *gloss-informed*, condensing the coordinated cut and passing step made explicit by the commentators; it should not be mistaken for a word-for-word translation of the compressed German couplets. In **12**, *Wechsler* is retained provisionally as “changer.” The RDL glosses explain the relevant tactical problem as the opponent's *Durchwechseln* (*changing through*): retaining the threatening point before the face or chest prevents that action in front of the point. I have therefore not silently substituted the gloss's fuller explanation for the Zettel's wording. In **13**, “quick strikes” is my provisional rendering of *Zeck*; related gloss wording (*Zeckrühr*) and other modern renderings warrant a short terminological note before final publication. “First cut” and the specific placement of the point before the face/chest are instructions developed in the **glosses**, not phrases in the Zettel itself.

## 2. Historical glosses

<p class="fff-label">Source summaries · attributed editorial wording</p>

The following are **editorial source summaries**, not direct quotations. Their purpose is to identify the relevant teaching and the differences between witnesses.

### 2.1 GNM Hs. 3227a — anonymous commentary

Begin with the left foot forward and threaten the opponent directly with a cut from the right once they can be reached by stepping. The opponent's head and body, rather than their sword, are the intended targets. The gloss emphasises movement, directness and avoiding unnecessary pauses. The closing step need not travel straight towards the opponent: it may also be taken obliquely towards a flank.

The surrounding exposition repeatedly stresses the importance of the point (*Ort*). It should remain directed towards the opponent's face or upper chest, maintaining an immediate threat along the shortest available path. The commentator also stresses continuous transitions when an action fails or is defended. In the subsequent discussion of *Vor*, the leading attack (*Vorschlag*) should be made as soon as distance allows. It occupies the opponent with defence; if a parry brings the swords into contact, the attacker should feel the bind and continue with the follow-up (*Nachschlag*) while the opponent remains occupied.

**Witness note:** 3227a's version of the *Common Lesson* includes additional lines, including advice to seek openings rather than strike at the sword. The explanation of *Vor / Vorschlag / Nachschlag* is relevant surrounding context, but extends beyond the selected verses 9–16.

**Source:** GNM Hs. 3227a, *Common Lesson* and surrounding exposition (approximately fols. 18r–24r); [Wiktenauer, anonymous long-sword gloss](https://wiktenauer.com/wiki/Pseudo-Hans_D%C3%B6bringer). Transcription: **Dierk Hagedorn**; English versions consulted in the earlier draft: **Michael Chidester (2022)** and **Christian Trosclair (2022)**. The wider folio range identifies supporting surrounding discussion, not a claim that verses 9–16 occupy it in full.

### 2.2 RDL — Ringeck, pseudo-Peter von Danzig and Lew

**Shared teaching.** All three glosses explain the coordination of cut and passing step in terms of reach, strength and balance. In *Zufechten*, the fencer should not wait passively to parry but enter decisively, cutting close to the opponent's head or body with the strength of the whole body. The point should remain threatening before the face or chest, restricting the opponent's ability to change through. The first cut should originate from the dominant side so the fencer remains capable of strong work in the resulting blade contact.

**Differences of emphasis and continuation:**

- **Ringeck** stresses promptly attacking the nearest opening if the swords bind.
- **Pseudo-Danzig** distinguishes responses according to the parry: strike or touch the arm if the opponent strongly displaces the point; if they raise their arms high, strike below to the body and step back.
- **Lew** explicitly describes the forceful opening cut as compelling the opponent to parry. After a strong parry, he instructs the fencer to strike the opponent's left arm and step back.

**References / editions:**

- **Ringeck:** [Wiktenauer, *Sigmund ain Ringeck*](https://wiktenauer.com/wiki/Sigmund_ain_Ringeck), *Common Lesson* §§3–7; Dresden transcription by **Dierk Hagedorn**, approximately fols. **11v–14v**; comparative English rendering by **Christian Trosclair** (2015, revised 2022).
- **Pseudo-Danzig:** [Wiktenauer, *Pseudo-Peter von Danzig*](https://wiktenauer.com/wiki/Pseudo-Peter_von_Danzig), *Common Lesson* §§4–9; Rome **Cod. 44.A.8**, approximately fols. **10r–11v**; transcription by **Dierk Hagedorn**; English renderings by **Cory Winslow (2016)** and **Christian Trosclair (2022)**.
- **Lew:** [Wiktenauer, *Lew*](https://wiktenauer.com/wiki/Lew), *Common Lesson* §§2–6; Augsburg transcription (beginning at **01v**) by **Dierk Hagedorn**; English renderings by **Cory Winslow (2016)** and **Christian Trosclair (2021)**.

### 2.3 Comparison

Both 3227a and RDL describe an initiative-taking entry that threatens the opponent rather than merely meeting their sword. Their continuations work with the opponent's defence, commonly through blade contact. Their emphases differ: 3227a develops continuous action and the *Vorschlag/Nachschlag* relationship in its broader exposition, while RDL offers more explicit cut-and-step instruction and particular responses to different parries. This comparison does **not** imply that every opponent must offer an identical, static defence.

## 3. My historical interpretation

<p class="fff-label">Author's reading · historical context</p>

**INTERPRETATION.** I read verses 9–16 primarily as an introduction to *Zufechten*: how to enter measure, initiate a credible attack and establish favourable conditions for subsequent tempos. I recognise that reading the footwork passage as a more general rule of correct cutting has merit and textual support. I do not dismiss that reading as wrong, but I disagree with treating it as the primary purpose of this entire passage. My reading places the technical instruction within the context of the opening: it builds a strong, long, balanced *Vorschlag*, rather than imposing one compulsory mechanical pattern on every cut in every phase of an exchange.

In this reading, I should enter the exchange with a committed, structurally sound attack directed at the opponent's head or upper torso. The corresponding step gives the action the reach, force and balance described in the glosses, while the threatening point creates an immediate danger for the opponent. If the attack connects, it may end the exchange. Otherwise, it creates a problem they must solve. The basic scenario emerging from the glosses is surprisingly straightforward: **I attack strongly, the opponent is driven to defend — typically through a parry — and the resulting situation offers opportunities to continue applying pressure while maintaining *Vor* through further tempos.**

**HYPOTHESIS — introductory teaching model.** I take **a forceful opening attack → the opponent has to defend, typically with the sword → exploitation of that defence** to be the simplest play through which this teaching introduces a broader tactical approach. It is an introductory scenario, not a complete model of fighting or proof that historical opponents always stood still. The wider gloss material introduces further reactions and problems as the teaching develops.

I read this sequence as a possible deliberate pedagogical simplification: first teach an effective opening and its typical response — a scenario which I suspect may have worked **as is**, potentially even against many opponents in Liechtenauer's time — then progressively add more difficult variations. 

One further observation: the mechanics of the **strong opening cut** closely resemble later descriptions of the *Zornhau*. I find the resemblance potentially useful as a teaching shortcut, but I do not believe that this passage should be interpreted as a *Zornhau executed in Vor*. Mechanical similarity does not imply an identical tactical role.

### A note on historical context and my interpretation

**AUTHOR'S CONSIDERED BELIEF / HYPOTHESIS.** I believe that defensive retreat was less frequent and less extensive in historical fencing contexts than it commonly is in modern competitive HEMA. This belief is informed by my reading of historical sources, broader period context, conversations with historical reenactors and personal fencing experience. It is not an established historical frequency estimate.

**MY POSSIBLE EXPLANATIONS, NOT CLAIMS BY THE SOURCES.** Two factors that I believe may have limited retreat are: contextual social expectations, and footwear. In a confrontation involving honour or satisfaction, retreating from an opponent's attack might have been interpreted as backing down from one's claim, rather than merely as a tactical withdrawal — potentially limiting a fencer's willingness to give substantial ground. Period footwear (imagine something like hardcore minimalist barefoot shoes), particularly on uneven or unfamiliar terrain, might also have made a rapid backward step onto unseen ground too risky to be common. The second suggestion is partly informed by conversations with modern reenactors who report taking particular care with their footing while wearing historical shoes.

**EMPIRICAL OBSERVATION — MY ACCOUNT.** My practical observation is straightforward: when I fence with an opponent who responds to a committed opening attack primarily through blade defence rather than retreating out of distance, Liechtenauer's subsequent plays work **exactly as the glosses describe them**. I mean this quite literally: the sequences unfold in accordance with the described instructions and expected outcomes. This observation informs my interpretation, but does not establish how commonly historical opponents responded in this manner.

**ON WITHDRAWAL.** None of the above means that historical fencers did not retreat, or that Liechtenauer prohibited backward movement. I distinguish an opponent-driven defensive withdrawal — particularly an abrupt retreat in immediate response to an incoming attack — from deliberately disengaging when an exchange no longer favours me, restoring distance and preparing another entry. The glosses themselves include backward steps and renewed attacks.

## 4. My application in modern HEMA

<p class="fff-label fff-label-modern">Author's practice · present-day conditions</p>

**MODERN MODEL / MY COMPETITIVE EXPERIENCE.** In my experience of competitive HEMA, the tactical environment often differs from the opening sequence described in the glosses. Against an attempted *strong, decisive entry*, opponents commonly respond with swift retreats, often accompanied by low-commitment attacks against the hands; they may also accept the risk of a double hit through a deliberate tactical counterattack. An opening cut delivered across the distance with a committed step therefore does not reliably produce the parry and subsequent advantageous bind anticipated by my introductory historical model.

I do not think this makes the underlying lesson obsolete. What I want to preserve is the tactical relationship: **I initiate an action that creates a credible problem; the opponent is forced to respond; and I work with that response at a timing (*tempo*) advantage.** The modern entry need not be the historical opening cut. Depending on the opponent, I might use a direct threat, a credible feint or a deliberate provocation to generate a response I can read, work with and exploit.

Note that the goal is not merely to make the opponent move. Their response must occur on terms that still leave me able to act. A reaction that stops my attack, takes away the initiative, or creates a double hit will not deliver the tactical advantage I am looking for. I therefore treat *Vor* as an advantage I must **establish and preserve through the next decision**, not an automatic reward for provoking *any* reaction. I consider this modern approach consistent with the tactical principle I infer from the glosses, while recognising that it achieves that principle through different means.
