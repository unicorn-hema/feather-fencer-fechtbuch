# Introduction to the Five Master Strikes — Zettel 21–22

<p class="fff-deck">Five hidden strikes, their exceptional place in the tradition, and what they may teach beyond the techniques themselves.</p>

## TL;DR

I see the five master strikes as something more than five particularly effective techniques. I think they occupy an exceptional position in Liechtenauer's teaching for three interconnected reasons.

First, they may have been a distinctive and easily recognisable feature of his fencing school. Second, their practical strength lies in their ability to threaten the opponent while simultaneously dealing with the opponent's threat (whether it is already present or arises in response to our attack). Third, I suspect that the five strikes may have served a broader pedagogical purpose: teaching five general mechanical principles that students could subsequently apply beyond the techniques themselves.

## 2. Historical glosses

<p class="fff-label">Source summaries · attributed editorial wording</p>

The following are summaries of the surviving explanations, not quotations. Although they differ in their approaches to these verses, all the sources agree on which five strikes are meant: Zornhau, Krumphau, Zwerchhau, Schielhau and Scheitelhau.

### 2.1 GNM Hs. 3227a — anonymous commentary

3227a introduces the five strikes but does not provide a dedicated explanation of these introductory verses. Instead, it moves directly towards the first of them, Zornhau. What's more, 3227a omits the second verse altogether—the one praising those who can defend against the five strikes.[^gloss-3227a]

### 2.2 RDL — Ringeck, pseudo-Peter von Danzig and Lew

The five strikes are presented as a recognised group of techniques. The RDL glosses attribute exceptional value to their knowledge; some explicitly suggest that many *other* masters are unfamiliar with them. The ability to overcome these five strikes is treated as an outstanding accomplishment worthy of recognition.

All three preserve the instruction to *learn them from the right side*, but their explanations of it differ.

**Ringeck**

Ringeck instructs the fencer to use these five strikes from the right side *against an opponent who stands and waits, prepared to defend himself*. He explicitly recommends attempting to hit that opponent with the **first strike**, using one of the five. A later version of this teaching preserved by Medel also specifically praises the ability to overcome not only the five strikes themselves, but their possible continuations.[^gloss-ringeck]

**Pseudo-Peter von Danzig**

Pseudo-Danzig instructs the reader to learn to execute them correctly from the right side, but does not develop an explicit tactical reason for this instruction or the same first-strike scenario found in Ringeck.[^gloss-danzig]

**Lew**

Lew likewise introduces five hidden strikes and praises the ability to overcome them through proper fencing without suffering injury. His explanation is considerably shorter. Although the Zettel retains the reference to the right hand, Lew's gloss does not expand on it.[^gloss-lew]

## 3. My historical interpretation

<p class="fff-label">Author's reading · historical context</p>

### 3.1 Terminology — Master Strikes

The term *master strikes* (*Meisterhäue*) is a later designation that does not appear in Liechtenauer's Zettel or the early glosses. Instead, the RDL glosses refer to *secret* or *hidden strikes* (*verporgene Häue*) and point to their exceptional standing within the art of fencing. The earliest use of the term *Meisterhäue* that I know of is in Joachim Meyer's work from 1570 (though there may well be earlier examples I am unaware of).

Interestingly, Meyer does not simply use a different name. He also goes beyond the early RDL glosses by offering a broader explanation of their significance: the five master strikes contain or give rise to other masterful techniques of fencing. I will return to this later in the chapter.[^meyer]

Nevertheless, the term *master strikes* is well established in modern HEMA, so I will use it throughout FFF as well.

### 3.2 The Right-Side Instruction

I find an interesting difference in how the glosses approach these introductory verses. While 3227a essentially passes over the introduction to the master strikes, Ringeck, Danzig and Lew all preserve the instruction to *strike from the right*. I see three possible explanations for this:

**First possibility — a teaching instruction**

The instruction concerns the learning process. The student should first learn to perform these five strikes from the right side. Once they have mastered them in that form, they may use them from other starting positions as well.

Such a reading would fit my broader understanding of Liechtenauer's system, as developed in the FFF [*Preface*](preface.md). In this case, the instruction would not be an absolute technical rule, but rather a recommended way to learn the techniques.

**Second possibility — a general technical rule**

The instruction does not concern the learning process, but the actual use of the strikes. In that case, the glosses would be saying that all five strikes should be performed from the right side.

On the one hand, this makes technical sense in the context of what we know—or think we know—about the early Liechtenauer tradition. On the other hand, it immediately produces an apparent inconsistency: for example, the glosses also describe performing Krumphau from *Schrankhut* on both sides.

**A third possibility — a tactical instruction for the opening attack**

In the case of Ringeck's gloss, I find this possibility quite convincing. His text connects the instruction with an opponent standing prepared to defend himself, and recommends attempting to hit with the first strike using one of these master strikes *from the right side*—just as the [*Zufechten*](zufechten.md) section told us to do. Only instead of a strong Oberhau, we are now encouraged to use one of the master strikes.

But which one? Well, the glosses are quite explicit about that too. We will find the answer later, in the *Vier Versetzen* section.

In this context, the instruction becomes a natural bridge between the general teaching on *Zufechten*, the master strikes introduced here and the later *Twelve Stücke*. And because it explicitly concerns the opening attack, it still leaves room for deviations from the *strike from the right* rule—deviations we find, for example, in the Krumphau section. There is also a good chance that, although only Ringeck makes this approach explicit, Danzig and Lew applied a similar logic without spelling it out in their respective glosses.

In the end, while the first and second explanations of the *from the right* instruction are still plausible, I believe the third option is the correct one.

### 3.3 A Distinctive Feature of Liechtenauer's School

I believe that the master strikes were a prominent and recognisable element of Liechtenauer's fencing system. The way the Zettel and particularly the RDL glosses speak of them suggests that the authors attached exceptional importance to them. At times, their presentation almost resembles modern marketing. I suspect that these five strikes may have been one of the elements that visibly distinguished Liechtenauer's school from other contemporary fencing traditions, especially in the eyes of outsiders.

They may have been techniques that were:

- sufficiently distinctive and recognisable;
- effective in practice;
- difficult or even impossible to reconstruct successfully without knowledge passed down by a master of the school;
- apparently characteristic of that school;
- and therefore well suited to demonstrating its fencing art.

This does not necessarily mean that Liechtenauer invented them, or even that no other tradition knew them. The important idea is that their exceptional position may have had not only technical significance, but also a role in the school's identity and presentation.

And this is where I find the second verse particularly interesting. The RDL glosses present these five strikes as exceptional knowledge, unfamiliar even to *many masters of the sword*. At the same time, they promise exceptional recognition to the fencer capable of overcoming them. The implicit message is almost promotional: **learn our exceptional techniques, and you gain an advantage that only a particularly capable opponent can hope to overcome.**

I also read this as a form of **art against art**. True fencing skill does not end with being able to execute an effective technique. It includes being able to overcome that same technique when used against you. The five strikes may therefore serve not only as a privileged offensive repertoire, but also as a rather convenient measure of fencing competence: one defined by the school's own techniques, naturally favouring its students and putting outsiders at a disadvantage.

## 4. My application in modern HEMA

<p class="fff-label fff-label-modern">Author's functional model · modern fencing</p>

For many modern HEMA practitioners, the master strikes are among the principal things they take away from Liechtenauer. In some HEMA schools, the understanding of the whole of KdF seems to begin and end with them. But in my view, the master strikes not only fall far short of representing the *whole* Liechtenauer school: they themselves bring several interconnected concepts together into a single whole.

### 4.1 Master Strikes as an Efficient Combination of Functions

I have already discussed one thing the master strikes share: the almost *marketing-like* role they seem to play in the tradition. But I think there is a practical reason for their prominence as well: their functional efficiency.

Master strikes are not just some 'good attacks'. Their essential feature is the ability to combine several functions within a single action. More specifically, they allow the fencer to threaten the opponent (or, ideally, to hit them directly) while simultaneously dealing with the opponent's threat. And **importantly, this defensive function is not limited to acting in *Nach*.**

Of course, *Nach* is the most obvious case: when I react to an incoming attack, my strike may simultaneously deal with that attack and threaten the opponent. But I can also attack in *Vor* and benefit from a defensive function against a potential counterattack, or prevent the opponent from attempting to take the initiative away from me. This is an important distinction. A technique may offer defensive security while being used offensively, and it may solve an immediate threat without surrendering the initiative.

In each case, what matters is that master strikes provide both offensive and defensive capabilities at the same time. To me, this corresponds very closely to what I consider the fundamental principles of Liechtenauer's system, particularly *Indes* and **decision compression**. In this sense, I understand the master strikes as an expression of practical efficiency: instead of a separate parry followed by a counterattack, they make it possible to combine several necessary functions within a single movement.

This theory also finds indirect support in the glosses: **four of the five master strikes later form the four *Versetzen***, corresponding to the four principal guards:[^versetzen]

| Master strike | Guard it counters |
|---|---|
| Zornhau | Not among the four *Versetzen* |
| Krumphau | Ochs |
| Zwerchhau | Vom Tag |
| Schielhau | Pflug |
| Scheitelhau | Alber |

Zornhau is the exception. Krumphau, Zwerchhau, Schielhau and Scheitelhau can all serve as active attacks against an opponent holding a specific guard and, as I will discuss later in the *Versetzen* chapter, provide a defence against the most threatening attack available from that guard—the most direct and fastest one. Zornhau-Ort, by contrast, is explicitly introduced as a specific response to an incoming Oberhau.

### 4.2 Five Strikes as Five Transferable Mechanical Principles

My third idea is considerably more speculative. It is something I can *see*, but for which I have no evidence beyond the fact that it makes sense to me, both on its own terms and in the context of the entire Liechtenauer system as I understand it.

I suspect that the five master strikes may not have represented merely five specific techniques, each intended to solve particular situations. They may also have served as **representatives of five general principles, which students learned through the strikes so that they could subsequently apply them beyond those specific techniques, as general mechanical principles**.

In this interpretation, each strike possibly represents a particular fundamental mechanic:

| Strike | General principle I see in it |
|---|---|
| **Zornhau** | **Force against force:** directly meeting and overcoming the opponent's incoming force. |
| **Zwerchhau** | **Closing a line:** denying the opponent's attack by closing the attacking line further away from the central line. |
| **Krumphau** | **Evasion:** moving the fencer out of the attacking line rather than dealing with the incoming force. |
| **Schielhau** | **Taking over the centre:** gaining control of the central line itself. |
| **Scheitelhau** | **Void:** removing the threatened target from the opponent's attacking line without having to relocate the entire fencer. |

Of course, the specific principle I associate with each master strike can be debated, and the list above may turn out to be wrong. That does not change how I see the underlying idea: each of these strikes is built around a different mechanic, and those mechanics can also be successfully applied beyond the particular strikes themselves. I have already discussed how I see Liechtenauer's system as a form of *compression*, and why I believe the Zettel and glosses are medieval alternatives to syllabuses and textbooks. Applying both of these ideas to the master strikes inevitably shapes how I read them in the context of the system and forms this whole idea.

There is also one interesting later parallel: Joachim Meyer explicitly presents the five master strikes as containing *further masterful techniques and skills*. This shows that some broader pedagogical understanding of the five strikes existed at least within the later Liechtenauer fencing tradition.[^meyer]

I am fully aware that I have no direct historical evidence for such a reading. I even doubt that it can ever be demonstrated conclusively. Further research may uncover reasons why this understanding is wrong and refute it. But I do not think it would be possible to establish its correctness definitively.

So, is my theory correct? I don't know. But do I find it useful and applicable to modern HEMA? You bet!
